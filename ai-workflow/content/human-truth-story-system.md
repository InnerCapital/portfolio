# Maya’s Human Truth Story System

This is the full process for turning a moment from Maya’s life into a strong social story.

The system starts with interrogation.

Do not write the story too early.

The event is rarely the story.

The meaning underneath the event is the story.

## Step 1: Pick the life pillar

Every story sits under one of four pillars.

### Love

Relationships, marriage, friendship, family, belonging, hobbies.

### Identity

Age, confidence, culture, faith, womanhood, reinvention, self-worth.

### Fitness

Body, health, energy, discipline, appearance, stress, ageing.

### Earnings

Business, career, money, status, ownership, ambition, success.

Some stories cross more than one pillar.

A business loss might start under Earnings but become a story about Identity.

A marriage story might start under Love but reveal something about ambition or independence.

Follow the emotion, not the category.

## Step 2: Start with one specific moment

Do not begin with a broad topic.

Weak:

> I want to talk about starting again.

Stronger:

> The first time I introduced myself as a creator after losing my business.

Ask:

- What happened?
- Where were you?
- Who was there?
- What happened immediately before?
- What did you say?
- What did you avoid saying?
- What did you feel in your body?
- What did you think nobody else noticed?

Specific scenes create stronger stories than general summaries.

## Step 3: Ask why three times

For every moment, ask:

> Why did that matter?

Then ask again:

> Why did that matter to you specifically?

Then ask again:

> What did that make you fear, question or realise?

Keep going until the answer moves away from the event and towards identity, love, safety, time, status, ambition or self-worth.

Example:

**What happened?**

I walked away from the business I spent five years building.

**Why did that matter?**

Because I had built my life around it.

**Why did that matter?**

Because being the founder had become part of how I saw myself.

**Why did that matter?**

Because without the business, I did not know whether people would still see me as successful.

Now we have the real story.

**The event:** Losing the business.

**The deeper meaning:** Losing an identity.

**The hidden fear:** Becoming ordinary after being successful.

**The human truth:** Achievement often becomes part of your identity before you notice.

## Step 4: Find the human truth

The human truth is the part of the story that belongs to everyone.

It should feel widely experienced but rarely articulated.

Ask:

- What do people pretend does not bother them?
- What contradiction are people living with?
- What are people scared to admit?
- What changes as people get older?
- What private thought sits underneath this moment?

Examples:

- People support your ambition until your ambition starts working.
- You can love your life and still want a different one.
- Starting again feels harder when people remember your first success.
- Some friendships do not end badly. They stop fitting.
- Comparison does not only steal joy. It ruins decision-making.
- The life you prayed for is still allowed to stop fitting.

## Step 5: Find the hidden fear

The human truth explains what people experience.

The hidden fear explains why they care.

Most hidden fears sit under these themes:

- Being left behind.
- Choosing the wrong life.
- Running out of time.
- Losing status.
- Being rejected.
- Being misunderstood.
- Failing publicly.
- Losing yourself.
- Wasting your potential.
- Losing what you built.
- Disappointing people.
- Succeeding and still feeling empty.

Ask:

- What was I scared this moment meant about me?
- What did I think I might lose?
- What did I worry other people would think?
- What future was I afraid of?
- What truth was I avoiding?

## Step 6: Find the tension

A story needs two beliefs fighting each other.

Without tension, the story becomes an update.

Common tensions in Maya’s content:

- Ambition versus peace.
- Success versus ownership.
- Stability versus freedom.
- Gratitude versus grief.
- Love versus independence.
- Past success versus starting again.
- Public perception versus private reality.
- Safety versus growth.
- Being understood versus choosing yourself.
- Looking successful versus feeling fulfilled.

Ask:

- What did I want?
- What did I need?
- What did I believe?
- What did life prove?
- What looked right from the outside?
- What felt wrong on the inside?

## Step 7: Find the personal receipt

The personal receipt is the lived experience that gives Maya the right to speak.

Strong receipts include:

- Starting a first business young.
- Growing that business to real scale.
- Stepping away from the company she built.
- Starting a creator career from scratch.
- Rebuilding publicly.
- Getting married during financial and professional uncertainty.
- Watching friends follow traditional careers.
- Turning 30 and questioning old timelines.
- Resting after years of survival mode.
- Moving out after building, losing and rebuilding.
- Thinking about business number two after losing business number one.

The receipt should support the point.

Do not force a big achievement into every story.

Sometimes the strongest receipt is a small moment.

A conversation with her partner.

A birthday.

A cancelled plan.

A quiet drive home.

A moment in the gym.

A comment from a family member.

The first invoice after a hard season.

## Step 8: Build through Safe, Real and Raw

Every story should move through three levels of depth.

### Safe

The observation most people agree with.

Example:

> A lot of us thought 30 would feel more organised.

### Real

The honest tension beneath the observation.

Example:

> Turning 30 made me realise I was still following deadlines I never chose.

### Raw

The fear people rarely say aloud.

Example:

> I was not scared of getting older. I was scared I had built the wrong life.

Safe gets attention.

Real builds connection.

Raw makes people feel seen.

Do not start raw without context.

Do not stay safe for the whole story.

## Step 9: Find Maya’s reframe

The reframe is the opinion earned from the experience.

This is where Maya’s voice enters.

Blunt.

Clear.

Slightly funny.

Emotionally intelligent.

Backed by receipts.

A good reframe changes the meaning of the event.

Examples:

- Taking risks young is front-loading the struggle.
- Being a late bloomer might save you from becoming successful at the wrong life.
- Starting again does not mean starting from zero. You restart with evidence.
- Comparison does not only steal joy. It makes you abandon good plans.
- You are not behind because your life looks different. You are behind when fear stops you moving.
- Losing the business did not remove the person who knew how to build one.
- The safe option still has a cost. You pay with the life you never test.

## Step 10: Turn the story back on the audience

End with a mirror.

The audience should leave thinking about their own life.

Avoid generic lessons.

Avoid “believe in yourself.”

Avoid tying every story into a neat bow.

Ask a question that creates self-reflection.

Examples:

- Do you still want the life you are working so hard to build?
- Are you behind, or are you building something that takes longer?
- Do you miss the old life, or the certainty you had inside it?
- What are you calling patience that is really fear?
- Are you protecting your peace or avoiding discomfort?
- What decision would you make if nobody needed to understand it?
- Are you scared to start again, or scared the next chapter might look smaller?

# The Final Story Structure

Use this order:

1. **Hook**  
   Open with the sharpest version of the human truth.

2. **Scene**  
   Take the audience into one specific moment.

3. **Belief**  
   Explain what Maya believed at the time.

4. **Collision**  
   Show what happened that challenged the belief.

5. **Fear**  
   Name what the moment made her question or fear.

6. **Reframe**  
   Share the new belief earned from the experience.

7. **Mirror**  
   Turn the story back on the audience.

# The Interview Process

Before writing, ask Maya these questions one at a time.

1. What is the exact moment you want to talk about?
2. What happened?
3. Why did that matter?
4. Why did that matter to you specifically?
5. Why did that matter underneath the obvious reason?
6. What did you fear that moment meant about you?
7. What did you want at the time?
8. What did you need instead?
9. What did you believe before this happened?
10. What do you believe now?
11. What part of this experience would other people recognise in themselves?
12. What is the uncomfortable truth nobody says about this?
13. What would someone disagree with?
14. What question should the audience ask themselves afterwards?

Do not rush through all the questions.

Stay with one answer when there is emotion underneath it.

Ask:

- Why?
- What made that painful?
- What did that represent?
- What were you scared would happen?
- What did you need people to understand?
- What did you not want to admit?

The goal is not to collect information.

The goal is to find the sentence beneath the sentence.

# Quality Control

Before writing, check:

- Is there one clear human truth?
- Is there a hidden fear beneath it?
- Did we use a specific moment?
- Did we ask why at least three times?
- Are two beliefs in conflict?
- Does Maya have a personal receipt?
- Is the reframe fresh and blunt?
- Does the ending make the audience examine their own life?
- Does the story sound lived, not taught?

The rule is simple.

If there is no tension, it is an update.

If there is no receipt, it is advice.

If there is no fear, it will struggle to connect.

If there is no reframe, there is no reason to share it.

If we have not asked why three times, we are not ready to write.
