# Reading and analysing a P&L

Turns a profit and loss statement into a short, honest read: what happened, why, whether the numbers can be trusted, and what to do next. Written for owner-run businesses (rental, hospitality, services, creators), where the P&L is usually produced by the bookkeeper in Xero or QuickBooks and nobody has checked how it was coded.

## Rules

1. **Never invent a number.** Every figure you state comes from the report or a calculation you show. If a figure isn't there, say so. Don't estimate a missing month.
2. **Check the books before you analyse them.** A clean-looking P&L built on miscoded transactions gives a confident wrong answer. Do the trust checks (below) first and report what you found.
3. **Compare, don't just describe.** A number alone means little. Every key line gets a comparison: prior period, same period last year, budget, or a per-unit figure.
4. **Separate one-offs from the run rate.** Strip out the unusual items before saying what the business earns in a normal month.
5. **Profit is not cash.** Say what the P&L can't show: deposits held, VAT owed, debtors, stock, loan repayments, capital spend.
6. **Lead with the conclusion.** The first line says what the reader should believe or do; the numbers support it.

## The structure (top to bottom)

| Line | What it is | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (turnover, sales) | Income earned in the period, net of VAT | Earned in this period, or just invoiced? Split by product/channel? |
| Cost of sales (direct costs) | Costs that rise with each sale: hire-in, haulage, materials, merchant fees, freelancers on a job | Are the right costs here, or sitting in overheads? |
| **Gross profit** | Revenue minus cost of sales | Gross margin % by line, and its trend |
| Overheads (operating expenses) | Rent, salaries, software, insurance, marketing, professional fees | Which are fixed, which discretionary, which grew fastest? |
| **EBITDA / operating profit** | Gross profit minus overheads (EBITDA before depreciation and amortisation) | Is the core business profitable before finance and accounting charges? |
| Depreciation, amortisation | The cost of assets spread over their life | Is it charged at all? Asset-heavy businesses often forget it |
| Interest, finance costs | Loans, asset finance, overdraft | Can operating profit cover it comfortably (interest cover)? |
| **Net profit before tax** | What's left | Then corporation tax |

## Order of work

### 1. Get the right report
- Period: a full month, quarter or year, on an **accrual** basis (cash-basis P&Ls hide timing problems). Ask for a comparison column (prior period and last year) and a budget if one exists.
- From Xero: the profit and loss report with comparison periods; tracking categories (by unit, site, product line) when the question is "which part makes money"; aged receivables and the balance sheet for the cash side; invoices or bank transactions to check how a line was coded.
- Note the basis, period, currency and whether figures are ex VAT.

### 2. Trust checks (report findings before analysis)
- **Deposits booked as revenue.** Refundable security deposits are a liability, not income. If they sit in a sales account, revenue and margin are overstated.
- **VAT on the wrong lines.** A no-VAT item (a refundable deposit) coded with VAT, or a VAT-able sale coded as exempt.
- **Income in the wrong period.** Advance payments for future events booked as this month's revenue (should be deferred and released when the hire or service happens).
- **Costs in the wrong place.** Job costs in overheads (inflates gross margin), or owner and personal costs in the business.
- **Missing lines.** No depreciation on owned kit, no accrual for a known bill, no wages in a month with staff.
- **Duplicates and test entries.** The same invoice twice, test invoices not voided, credit notes left open.
- **Suspense or uncategorised balances**, and large round "journal" entries without a description.
- **Sign errors.** Negative revenue or negative costs on a line that should never go below zero.
List each finding with the account, the amount and the effect on profit. If a finding is material, analyse both as reported and as corrected, and label which is which.

### 3. The core numbers
Calculate and show the working:
- Revenue growth % (vs prior period and vs last year)
- Gross margin % = gross profit / revenue, overall and by line or tracking category
- Overheads as % of revenue, and overhead growth vs revenue growth
- EBITDA margin and net margin %
- Break-even revenue = fixed overheads / gross margin %
- Interest cover = operating profit / interest
- Unit economics where there's a natural unit: revenue, gross profit and utilisation per asset, per job, per customer or per head

### 4. Explain the movement
Build a bridge from last period's profit to this period's: volume, price, mix, direct cost rate, each big overhead line, one-offs. Name the two or three drivers that explain most of the change, with figures. Check seasonality before calling a trend: compare with the same month last year, not just last month.

### 5. Normalise
Remove one-offs (a legal bill, a grant, a big one-off sale, a write-off) and show the underlying run rate. Annualise only full, representative months, and say which months you used.

### 6. What the P&L doesn't show
Cash position, money owed to and by the business, deposits held, VAT due, loan capital repayments, capital spend. If the question is "can we afford X", the answer needs the balance sheet and cash flow too; say so.

## Industry notes

- **Rental and hire:** revenue is earned over the hire period, so long hires spanning a month end need splitting. Deposits are liabilities. Utilisation (hire days / available days) and revenue per unit matter more than total revenue. Depreciation on the fleet is a real cost even when the kit is paid for.
- **Hospitality and events:** gross margin by event, cost of sales including staff on the day, heavy seasonality.
- **Services and agencies:** gross margin after freelancers and direct staff time; revenue per head.
- **Creators:** split brand deals, platform payouts and products; agency fees are cost of sales; payment terms make cash lag the P&L by months.

## Output format

1. **Headline:** one or two sentences: the conclusion and the single most important number.
2. **Can we trust it:** trust-check findings, each with account, amount and effect on profit. "No issues found" only if you actually checked.
3. **Key numbers:** a small table of the core numbers vs comparison.
4. **What drove it:** the two or three drivers, with figures.
5. **Underlying run rate:** after one-offs.
6. **Risks and blind spots:** what the P&L can't tell us here.
7. **Actions:** at most five, each owned and specific (recode X, chase Y, review price of Z).

Keep it to a page unless asked for more. Use a chart only when a trend over time is the point.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Avoid it by |
|---|---|
| Analysing miscoded books as if they were right | Trust checks first, both versions when material |
| "Revenue is up, so we're doing well" | Margin, overhead growth and cash alongside revenue |
| Calling a seasonal swing a trend | Compare with the same period last year |
| Annualising one strong month | Use full representative months and say which |
| Treating deposits or advance payments as income | Check the revenue accounts' transactions |
| Advice beyond the evidence | Each action cites the line and figure behind it; flag anything needing an accountant (tax, VAT treatment, audit) |

This is analysis, not accounting or tax advice. Point to the accountant for VAT treatment, tax, statutory accounts and anything that changes what is filed.
